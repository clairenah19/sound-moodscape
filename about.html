Moodscape

소리로 느끼는 한국 — Feeling Korea Through Sound
A data sonification project exploring whether geographic and demographic data can be translated into music that communicates the character of a place.
📍
What's actually running vs. what's proposed. This page separates the parts of Moodscape that exist in the live app (the interactive map) from the research concept and roadmap items that are still design proposals. Badges on each section show the current status.

01 Research question

Can geographic and demographic data be converted into music that lets a listener understand the character of a place — without seeing a map?

Most geographic tools communicate visually: maps, charts, colour gradients. Moodscape investigates whether sound can carry the same information, and whether a piece of music can feel like a more emotionally accurate representation of a place than a number on a chart.

Hypothesis: a listener who hears a Moodscape sonification for an unfamiliar province, without being told which one it is, will correctly guess its relative population density, energy level, and safety at a rate above chance.

02 Why sonification

Sonification — turning data into non-speech audio — sits at the intersection of computer science, cognitive psychology, and data science. Geographic sonification specifically is under-explored, and it addresses a real accessibility gap: maps, heat maps, and choropleth charts are inaccessible to blind and low-vision users, a limitation documented by prior academic work such as the University of Maryland's iSonic project (ACM TOCHI, 2008) and a 2024 systematic review noting the lack of live, assessed sonification tools for this group. Instrumentation also carries cultural meaning a bare statistic can't: a gayageum playing a slow phrase signals "ancient and still" in a way the number 48 does not.

03 The sonification algorithm Transparent activity proxy live — validation still required

What's live now: official-data activity proxy

All 17 regions use the same five province-level inputs: 2025 population density, 2024 domestic-tourism trips per resident, 2023 noise complaints per 100,000 residents, registered noise-emitting facilities per 100,000 residents, and crowding pressure derived from the official 2024 National Tourism Survey. Each positive input is log transformed and min–max scaled across the 17 regions before weighting, so a single extreme value cannot automatically decide the result.

Activity Proxy = 35% density + 30% tourism intensity + 15% noise complaints + 10% noise facilities + 10% crowding pressure

A second, non-audio context score is kept separate: Visitor Pleasantness = 40% overall satisfaction + 25% recommendation + 20% revisit intention + 15% crowding satisfaction. Keeping activity and pleasantness separate follows the logic of the ISO 12913 circumplex without claiming that tourism responses are an ISO soundscape survey.

The inputs are measured; the weights are human-chosen starting hypotheses. This score is therefore reproducible but not yet validated as perceived Eventfulness. Direct Seoul and Daejeon soundscape findings appear only as comparison references and are not blended into the score. Place-level values remain illustrative offsets because equivalent landmark-level data do not exist.

Why these specific percentages — the live formula's weights

Each weight below is argued from how much a variable actually differentiates the 17 regions from each other and how directly it measures what it's standing in for — the same two questions the historical four-variable formula's weights (further down this section) were argued from, applied here to the formula that actually runs.

Density at 35%, the largest weight in the Activity Proxy. Of the five inputs, density has by far the widest real spread — from about 90/km² in Gangwon to over 15,400/km² in central Seoul, more than two orders of magnitude. It's also the most stable and objective of the five: a census figure, not a self-report subject to mood or memory. The input that both discriminates hardest between regions and is measured most reliably earns the top weight.

Tourism intensity at 30%, second because it corrects a blind spot density has. Trips-per-resident is what stops a structurally sparse province like Gangwon (90/km²) from reading as dead quiet — Gangwon is actually Korea's most tourism-saturated province per capita, at roughly 20 trips per resident per year, the widest relative spread of any input. Without this term, low-density-but-tourist-flooded regions would be scored purely on emptiness. It sits just under density rather than above it because a trip count is a seasonal flow that moves year to year, while density is a structural fact — the more volatile measure takes the second seat, not the first.

Noise complaints per 100k at 15%. A genuine measured signal, but a behavioral one before it's an acoustic one — it depends on whether residents in a region actually file a complaint, not purely on how loud things are. Its spread across regions (roughly 9×, Sejongsi to Seoul) is real but noticeably narrower than density's or tourism's. A real but noisier-as-a-construct, moderately-discriminating input sits in the middle of the weighting, not at either end.

Noise-emitting facilities per 100k at 10%. This one has a large raw spread (roughly 75×) but it doesn't move with the other four inputs — Seoul, the highest-density and highest-tourism region, has the lowest facility count per capita, while an industrial province like Chungcheongbuk-do tops the list. It's measuring industrial character, not crowd energy — a genuinely different signal worth including, but one that could pull a quiet manufacturing province's score up in a way that would mislead if trusted too heavily. Low weight lets it nudge the result, not decide it.

Crowding pressure at 10%, tied for smallest. Across all 17 regions this is the narrowest band of any Activity Proxy input — under 2× from lowest to highest, since every province's visitors report fairly similar crowding satisfaction. Following the same logic used for temperature in the historical formula below: a near-uniform variable is capped at the smallest weight so it can't dilute the inputs actually doing the work of telling regions apart.

Overall satisfaction at 40%, the largest Visitor Pleasantness weight, despite the narrowest spread of its four inputs. It only ranges about 5.5 points across all 17 regions on a 100-point scale — the tightest band in either formula. It still leads because it's the single most direct answer available to "was being here pleasant," the headline item the underlying survey is built around. Directness of measurement outweighs the fact that its numbers barely move region to region.

Recommendation at 25%. A stronger, less self-serving signal than a passive satisfaction rating — recommending a place to others is a costlier, more honest endorsement — and its spread (roughly 8.3 points) is wider than overall satisfaction's. Second-most-direct measure, second-largest weight.

Revisit intention at 20%. Logically adjacent to recommendation but personal rather than social: a visitor can genuinely find a place pleasant without ever needing to return — a memorial park or a one-time landmark, for instance. Its spread (roughly 9.0 points) is technically the widest of the three headline Pleasantness items, but its link to "was this pleasant" is weaker for certain place types, which is why it sits a notch below recommendation rather than above it.

Crowding satisfaction at 15%, smallest. It has the widest raw spread of all four Pleasantness inputs (roughly 11.4 points) but is the narrowest in scope — a single facet of experience, not a holistic read. It's also already doing work on the other side of the model, inverted into "crowding pressure" for the Activity Proxy. Keeping it smallest here stops one raw survey question from carrying outsized weight on both axes of the model at once.

📎
Honesty note: this per-weight reasoning was written after the formula, from the actual data spreads and each variable's directness and redundancy against the others — it documents a defensible rationale for the numbers above, not a record of the original decision process. Like the historical formula's weights below, none of this is claimed to be the optimal split, and it hasn't been tested against a listener or perception study.

The four-variable formula below was Moodscape's original scoring design (Safety, Language proficiency, Density, Annual Temperature). It is retained as design history and does not produce the live region score. Density and Seasonal Drift remain possible independent audio parameters:

Mood Score (four-variable, proposed) = (Safety × 0.30) + (Language proficiency × 0.25) + (Density Score × 0.25) + (Annual Temp Score × 0.20)

The second term used to be fixed as "English proficiency," sourced from the EF EPI — which quietly assumed every listener is, or wants to be understood in, English. That's now generalized to Language proficiency: the live app lets a listener pick their own language from a dropdown in each province's panel (English plus eleven others — Mandarin, Japanese, Spanish, French, German, Vietnamese, Russian, Arabic, Hindi, Portuguese, Thai), and estimates that specific province's accessibility for the chosen language on the spot via a Gemini API call — the same bring-your-own-key mechanism already used by "Ask a Local" and the AI music-style predictor, so no new key is needed if one's already saved. English is the only language with a real citable index (EF EPI) behind it, so the AI is told to anchor on that number when English is selected; every other language gets an explicitly labeled reasoned estimate built from proxy signals (tourism volume, expat communities, signage prevalence) — the same estimate-what's-uncited-rather-than-invent-it approach documented for the research pipeline in section 05. Results are cached per province/language pair in the browser so they're not re-queried on every visit.

That score drives four audio parameters, plus a fifth — Seasonal Drift — computed independently from the province's temperature swing rather than the composite score:

🎵 Pitch
A4 (440 Hz) at score 0 → A5 (880 Hz) at score 100, roughly 1–2 semitones per 10 points.
📊 Waveform height
Bars in the player grow with score — tall and dense for high-energy places, short and spacious for calm ones.
🥁 Tempo
40 BPM (meditative) at score 0 to 120 BPM (driving) at score 100, mapped linearly.
🎻 Timbre
0–35: traditional acoustic (gayageum, daegeum, haegeum). 36–65: ambient-electronic hybrid. 66–100: synth, electric bass, digital beats.
🍂 Seasonal Drift — new
Pitch/timbre arcs from a cooler "winter" register toward a brighter "summer" one and partway back over the piece's runtime, scaled by that province's actual annual temperature swing.

Why this weighting — the judgment call behind the formula

The formula isn't arbitrary, but it isn't derived from a regression or a listener study either — it's a stated design judgment, and it's worth being explicit about the reasoning rather than presenting four numbers as if they were self-evidently correct. Two constraints shaped every input before any weight was assigned: each factor had to map onto a documented axis of how people actually perceive places (ISO 12913's Pleasantness/Eventfulness split, discussed in section 02, plus accessibility and physical comfort), and each had to have a real, cited, apples-to-apples statistic across every province — Numbeo, EF EPI, KOSIS, KMA — so the score is built from sourced numbers rather than invented ones.

Safety at 30%, the largest single weight. Of the four inputs, safety is the one that can flip a place's whole character rather than just shading it — a dense, beautiful city that felt unsafe wouldn't read as "exciting," it would read as "tense." That qualitative asymmetry, plus the fact that the Numbeo Safety Index is the most consistently sourced, directly comparable number across every province, is why it leads the formula rather than sharing the top weight with something else.

Language proficiency at 25%. This one is a judgment call specific to who Moodscape is for: a listener discovering a Korean province through sound for the first time, without local context, skews toward someone who doesn't speak Korean — but assuming that "someone" always means an English speaker was its own quiet bias, worth naming rather than leaving baked in. Fixing it to English also threw away information every other listener could get: it's now audience-selectable and computed per language, so how navigable and welcoming a place feels is a first-order input tailored to the actual listener, not a footnote borrowed from one specific one.

Density at 25%, matched deliberately to language proficiency. Density is the closest available proxy for the Eventfulness half of ISO 12913 — energy, crowding, pace. It's set equal to language proficiency rather than lower so that neither becomes a rounding error next to Safety's 30%, while still leaving Safety as the one factor allowed to dominate outright.

Annual temperature at 20%, the smallest weight, on purpose. Of the four inputs, temperature has by far the narrowest real spread across Korean provinces — the four reference provinces cluster within about 5°C of each other on annual average, versus density's two-order-of-magnitude range. Weighting a near-uniform variable any higher would dilute the three inputs that actually differentiate provinces from one another. It stays in the formula as a genuine comfort signal — it isn't dropped — but it's sized to match how little differentiating work it's actually doing at the annual-average level, which is also the reason Seasonal Drift was pulled out as its own, separately-weighted audio dimension below rather than folded into this static score.

The same principle — bound each term by a defensible real-world anchor rather than a round number picked for convenience — carries into the Density Score curve and the Seasonal Drift clamp immediately below: the density ceiling is Seoul's actual cited peak, not an arbitrary 100, and the 30°C drift denominator is the widest documented provincial swing, not a guess. None of this is claimed to be the optimal weighting — it's the project's best current, justified judgment, and it's explicitly falsifiable: the listening-test roadmap item in section 06 is what would actually test whether 30/25/25/20 tracks real listener perception better than some other split, or worse.

Density Score — named zones, not a raw inverse

Korean provincial density spans more than two orders of magnitude (≈50/km² in the emptiest counties to 16,000/km² in central Seoul), so a linear scale would flatten everything except Seoul to near-zero. Density Score uses a log curve anchored between a floor of 50/km² ("empty") and a ceiling of 18,000/km² (just above Seoul's cited peak, "overcrowded"):

Density Score = 100 × (log₁₀(density) − log₁₀(50)) / (log₁₀(18,000) − log₁₀(50)), clamped to [0, 100]

That continuous score is then labelled into six named zones, the same way the app already buckets mood scores into "Calm" / "Balanced" / "Lively":

0–15 · Empty
Remote countryside — near-zero foot traffic, silence dominates.
15–35 · Sparse
Rural provinces, small towns, farmland.
35–55 · Comfortable
Suburbs and small cities — room to breathe, still populated.
55–75 · Bustling
Urban districts, active streets, visible crowds.
75–90 · Dense
Major metro areas — constant activity, tight blocks.
90–100 · Overcrowded
Megacity core — maximum foot traffic, near-continuous crowding.

Seasonal Drift — the temperature swing, not just the average

The original formula reduced each province's climate to a single annual average, which erases Korea's four sharply distinct seasons. Every province already has cited January (winter) and August (summer) normals — their difference is the Seasonal Swing, and it drives a genuinely new audio dimension rather than folding into the static mood score:

Seasonal Swing = August avg temp − January avg temp Drift Depth = Seasonal Swing / 30°C (clamped to [0, 1], ~30°C ≈ the widest realistic Korean provincial swing)

A province with a wide swing gets a piece that audibly evolves — the pitch centre and timbre brightness ramp up through a simulated "spring→summer" arc across the first half of the 15 seconds, then ease back down through "autumn→winter" in the second half. A province with a narrow swing stays comparatively steady throughout. Spring and autumn aren't independently sourced here — they're modelled as the linear transition between the cited winter and summer normals, which is an honest simplification, not independent KMA seasonal data.

Province-level activity scores in the live app are computed from the official-data composite above, not this historical four-variable design. Place-level scores remain illustrative, and neither the historical Density Score nor Seasonal Drift is independently wired into the audio yet.

Audio: real AI tracks (Suno), with a synth fallback

Each place resolves its soundscape through three layers, in order: (1) a pre-generated real track — a hosted URL, or an MP3 dropped into the audio/ folder (generated in Suno from a per-place prompt the app builds automatically); (2) fully automatic live generation if a paid sunoapi.org API key is saved in the browser — request/response shape verified against their published API docs, not guessed; (3) a built-in Web Audio synth fallback so the player is never silent. The prompts are deliberately genre-distinct by mood band — a "very peaceful" place asks Suno for slow ambient drone, a "very exciting" one for driving EDM — so tracks differ sharply from each other, not just in pitch. When a real track plays it's routed through a live audio analyser, so the waveform reacts to the actual audio. Suno itself has no official public API, which is why layer (2) depends on a paid third-party provider rather than Suno directly; whether that provider's server allows direct browser-origin requests (CORS) is unverified, so the download-and-drop workflow in layer (1) remains the dependable path if live generation fails silently against CORS.

05 Research pipeline & process Documented methodology

How the region-level research behind Moodscape — the related-work survey and the fused City Mood & Character Report — was actually produced, so the process is auditable and repeatable, not just the output.

This is a methods report, not a findings report. It covers six stages: literature search, framework selection, parallel data compilation, fusion, narrative synthesis, and service integration.

1

Literature search

Before collecting any new data, a scoping search across ScienceDirect, ACM Digital Library, arXiv, and PLOS One checked whether published research already covers Moodscape's core idea. No paper did exactly that, but it surfaced close precedents — Chatty Maps (Aiello et al., 2016), the ISO 12913 soundscape standard, real Korean urban-soundscape studies of Seoul and Daejeon, place-identity/city-branding research, and iSonic (2008). Delivered as a standalone document first, since it determines what framing the rest of the pipeline can honestly use.

2

Framework selection

The brief called for a named psychology framework, with Russell's Circumplex Model suggested as a starting point. ISO 12913 — Russell's model formally adapted for environmental/soundscape research, with Pleasantness and Eventfulness axes and eight standard descriptors — was identified as the stronger fit, since it's the instrument the actual soundscape research community uses and several Stage 1 studies report results in that exact format. The substitution was flagged and confirmed before it was used to structure the rest of the project.

3

Parallel data compilation

Covering all 17 Moodscape regions on two axes (emotional and cultural) was split three ways — six regions, six regions, five — and run as three parallel research passes under an identical brief: cite a directly published region-specific soundscape study if one exists; otherwise produce an explicitly labeled reasoned estimate from real proxy data (industrial character, density, tourism, terrain) rather than inventing precise numbers; and compile cultural facts with specific numbers, dates, and named things, each cited.

4

Fusion

The two data types were combined but deliberately kept in different formats: emotional data as a 0–100 Pleasantness/Eventfulness ratio, visualized as a circumplex scatter chart and table with sourced/estimated status color- and shape-coded; cultural/character data as prose rather than a graph, since cultural identity resists being reduced to a single number the way a soundscape survey response can be.

5

Narrative synthesis — the LLM step

The compiled raw facts and citations from Stage 3 were turned into 17 flowing paragraphs, each following the same structure: state the region's Pleasantness/Eventfulness position and its sourcing status, then weave in 2–4 real, cited facts into coherent narrative rather than a bullet list — with every claim traceable back to Stage 3's sourcing in the report's reference list.

6

Service integration

The fused findings rewrote the 17 region-level taglines shown in the live app, grounding generic phrases in specific researched facts. Its reasoned P/E estimates are no longer used as measured data. The live color and audio baseline now come from the reproducible official-data composite in section 03; Seoul and Daejeon research is retained only as external comparison evidence. Place-level scores remain illustrative because no equivalent per-landmark dataset exists.

What the process turned up

2 of 17
Regions with a directly published soundscape study (Seoul, Daejeon) — a real finding about how sparse this literature is, not an assumption going in.

Almost every region also split into at least two distinct characters rather than one uniform identity — Ulsan's whaling heritage under its shipyards, Incheon's Chinatown beside Songdo, Ansan's Baekje temples near a Hyundai plant. Several regions' scores in the fused report are explicitly noted as averages of two different local profiles rather than a single clean read.

📎
What this pipeline is not: a claim that it constitutes a validated scientific methodology. It's a documented, repeatable procedure for compiling and fusing publicly available data under explicit honesty constraints — cite what's real, label what's estimated — stated plainly rather than implied to be more rigorous than it is. Full writeup: research/Moodscape_Process_Pipeline_Report_EN.pdf.

06 Roadmap Proposed — not yet built

Reconciled against what's actually in the app (DEVELOPMENT_PLAN2.md housekeeping item) — two of these five turned out to be partly built already, not proposals sitting at zero.

🌡️ Live weather-to-pitch

Pull today's actual temperature per province from the free Open-Meteo API and offset the baseline pitch/tempo from it, so the same province sounds different on a hot day than a cold one. Not started.

🔬 User study — protocol ready, fieldwork pending

A structured listening test (sighted and blind/low-vision participants) to check whether people can correctly infer density, safety, and temperature from the sonification alone, above chance. The ISO Pleasantness/Eventfulness calculation, the Korean participant questionnaire, and the scatter-plot analysis (research/iso_pe_calculator.js, research/soundwalk_questionnaire_ko.md) are built and ready; only the physical site visits remain, which can't be automated.

🤖 AI mood narrative generator — deterministic version live

narrative.js already generates a non-repeating text description of every place and province's soundscape from its live score, note, tempo, key, and instrumentation — instantly, with no API key. What's still proposed is swapping that deterministic template for actual language-model phrasing, the way the AI music-style predictor already uses Gemini.

📦 Open dataset — largely published

The research/ folder already publishes the official-data inputs, the Activity Proxy and Visitor Pleasantness formulas, external source citations, and the validation protocol as CSVs and markdown. Still missing: the resulting per-place musical parameters (pitch/BPM/key/timbre) for all 17 regions as one downloadable table, rather than only visible inside the live app.

07 Improvements needed Built, but short of the original design

Tracked separately from the roadmap (section 06), which is for features that don't exist at all yet. This section is for a feature that's live in the app but doesn't yet do everything it was meant to.

Accessibility

The design was corrected, not just extended. The first version put accessibility behind a separate toggle that spoke through the Web Speech API and captured the arrow keys globally. That competes with a blind visitor's actual screen reader — VoiceOver, NVDA or JAWS — which already has the visitor's chosen voice, speed and braille display, and which uses those same arrow keys for its own navigation. The map itself is now accessible, and the guided audio tour is an extra on top rather than the only way in.

What's built:

  • The map is a set of real controls. All 17 provinces are tab stops with role="button" and an aria-label carrying the region name, its modelled activity score, its mood label and whether a direct soundscape study exists behind it. Tab reaches them, Enter and Space open them, and the visitor's own screen reader reads them in their own voice. A focus outline follows each province's actual shape.
  • Selections are announced through one small live region rather than by re-reading the whole panel, which would otherwise repeat every control, prompt and API-key field on each click.
  • The navigation cue now carries the data. Focusing a province plays that province's own modelled activity as a pitch on a pentatonic scale, so tabbing across the country traces its activity contour as a melody instead of repeating one fixed beep. Because the reformed proxy compresses real scores into roughly 33–61 out of 100, the pitch range is stretched across the observed spread rather than the theoretical 0–100 — mapping the full range would collapse all 17 regions onto four pitches, nine of them identical.
  • Deaf and hard-of-hearing visitors have a way in. Every place now carries a "The music, without hearing it" panel giving the exact parameters the synth uses — pitch and frequency, tempo with a pulse at the real BPM, key, timbre family, density and instrumentation — read from the same functions that drive the audio, so the two cannot drift apart.
  • Untranslated text declares its own language. With the interface set to Korean, text still awaiting translation is marked lang="en" so a Korean screen reader voice does not attempt to read English words.
  • Motion is opt-out. The tempo pulse, waveform animation and the guided tour's pulsing ring all stop under prefers-reduced-motion; the tempo stays readable as text either way.

What's still missing:

  • Stereo/spatial positioning — moving east or west across the map still doesn't pan the audio, so the map has no physical sense in audio space. This is the next piece, and iSonic (section 08) is the precedent for it.
  • Directional movement — the guided tour still steps through a fixed list of 17 rather than moving north, south, east or west from wherever you are, so it doesn't teach the country's geography.
  • Full stat readout — the announcement covers name, score and mood label, but not the underlying official inputs (density, tourism, complaints, facilities, crowding) that section 03's proxy is built from.
  • No user testing yet — none of this has been tried with blind or low-vision users, so it remains a set of informed guesses. Everything above is a correction made from standards and published practice, not from watching someone use it. That is what the "User study" roadmap item (section 06) is meant to close, and it is the item that matters most.

08 Related work

NASA — Black Hole Sonification (2022)

NASA's Chandra X-ray Centre mapped pressure waves from the Perseus galaxy cluster into audible sound by scaling them 57 octaves upward, with X-ray brightness as pitch and distance from the black hole as stereo position. Patterns invisible in the raw image became audible in seconds. Moodscape borrows the same basic move — spatial/geographic data mapped directly to audio parameters.

iSonic — Geographic Sonification for Blind Users (ACM TOCHI, 2008)

The University of Maryland's HCI Lab built an interactive sonification tool letting blind users explore georeferenced data through coordinated sound and speech; testing with blind participants showed they could identify facts and trends in unfamiliar geographic data by ear. It's the closest academic precedent for Moodscape's accessibility mode.
